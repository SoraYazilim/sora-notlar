Ask any network team about their firewall and one complaint comes up again and again: the device never seems as fast in production as the datasheet suggested. In most cases nothing is broken. The gap comes from comparing real-world traffic against a number that was never meant to describe it.

FortiGate datasheets list throughput separately for raw firewall, IPS, threat protection, and SSL inspection. The raw firewall figure is measured with no UTM features enabled, so it says little about everyday use. The value closest to reality is the Threat Protection throughput, measured with antivirus and IPS turned on — and once SSL inspection enters the picture, effective throughput drops further. Sizing a model and setting expectations around Threat Protection numbers prevents most performance disappointments before they happen.

Let the hardware do the heavy lifting

The foundation of FortiGate performance is hardware acceleration. Eligible traffic is offloaded to NP (Network Processor) and CP (Content Processor) chips, so many sessions place almost no load on the CPU. The catch: certain configurations — some UTM combinations, or features incompatible with offload — pull traffic back to the CPU, and performance suffers. Verifying offload status and disabling unnecessary features that break it can raise throughput significantly without buying new hardware.

UTM inspection is a balancing act rather than an on/off switch. Flow-based inspection suits high-volume traffic that needs low latency, while proxy-based mode fits sensitive traffic that demands deeper control. SSL deep inspection offers strong protection but is among the costliest operations, so it should be applied selectively instead of everywhere.

Policy hygiene matters too: removing unused rules, moving frequently matched rules toward the top, using narrow address and service objects, and logging only what is necessary all reduce evaluation load. On top of that, continuous monitoring of CPU, memory, session counts, and conserve mode alerts catches bottlenecks early — and keeping firmware current brings the performance improvements shipped with new releases, tested before production of course.
