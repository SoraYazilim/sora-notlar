Most AI teams do not outgrow their hardware overnight. It happens gradually: a shared calendar appears for booking GPU time, a fine-tuning run gets postponed because a colleague is training, and the demo endpoint goes dark outside office hours. None of these are hardware failures — they are signs that single-user workstations have reached their organizational limit and that the team needs a shared GPU server.

A GPU server is a rack-mounted, dual-CPU platform housing four to eight or more enterprise GPUs. Unlike a workstation, it stays online continuously and serves multiple users at once through API endpoints. A typical configuration pairs high-core-count Intel Xeon or AMD EPYC processors with 512 GB to 6 TB of ECC RAM and fast NVMe storage, while dedicated IPMI/BMC management cards provide permanent remote access.

Matching silicon to workload

Choosing the right accelerator depends on what you run. H100 (80 GB HBM3) and H200 (141 GB HBM3e) are optimized for heavy model training; A100 remains a balanced option for training and inference; L40S and A40 (48 GB GDDR6) deliver better price-to-performance for inference-focused workloads. The interconnect matters just as much: NVLink 4.0 gives H100/H200 up to 900 GB/s of direct GPU-to-GPU bandwidth, while PCIe 5.0 x16 provides 64 GB/s bidirectional. On the host side, AMD EPYC 9005 (Turin) offers up to 160 PCIe 5.0 lanes per socket — enough to feed eight GPUs at full x16 speed.

Sharing that hardware safely is a solved problem. NVIDIA MIG partitions a single H100 or A100 at the hardware level into up to seven isolated instances, each with its own protected memory and compute. Inference frameworks such as Triton Inference Server and vLLM can then host models of different sizes — 7B, 13B, 70B parameters — as independent API endpoints on one physical machine.

The economics reward planning, too. IDC reports worldwide AI infrastructure spending reached $318 billion in 2025, more than double the $153 billion of 2024, with the market projected to pass $1 trillion by 2029. For continuous workloads, an on-premise deployment typically amortizes within 18–24 months and adds data sovereignty and predictable costs. Power deserves equal attention: a single 8-GPU NVIDIA DGX B200 can draw up to roughly 14.3 kW, and NVIDIA states it delivers 3X the training and 15X the inference performance of the previous-generation DGX H100.
